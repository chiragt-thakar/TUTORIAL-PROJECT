An eigenvector of `A` is a nonzero direction that `A` does not rotate, only scales; the scale factor
is its eigenvalue. If a matrix has a full set of these directions, working in that basis turns the
matrix into a diagonal one, and hard questions — matrix powers, long-run behaviour, principal
components — become scalar arithmetic.

That change of basis is the eigendecomposition `A = V Lambda V_inv`. Not every matrix has one, the
characteristic polynomial is the wrong way to find it numerically, and symmetric matrices are the
well-behaved special case the next lesson builds on.

<TypeScriptComparison>

- JavaScript has no eigenvalue routine; for `2x2` you solve the quadratic `lambda^2 - trace*lambda + det = 0` by hand.
- Power iteration — repeatedly multiply a vector by `A` and renormalise — is a few lines in any language and finds the dominant eigenvector; it is what PageRank runs.
- `np.linalg.eig` returns complex arrays even for a real matrix, because real matrices can have complex-conjugate eigenvalue pairs (rotations); TypeScript code would need an explicit complex type.
- `np.linalg.eigh` is the symmetric-matrix version: real eigenvalues, orthonormal eigenvectors, faster and more accurate — always use it when you know the matrix is symmetric.

</TypeScriptComparison>

## The defining equation

`A v = lambda v` with `v != 0`. The vector `v` keeps its direction under `A` and is scaled by the
scalar `lambda`. Rearranged, `(A - lambda*I) v = 0`, so `v` is a nonzero null-space vector of
`A - lambda*I`, which forces `det(A - lambda*I) = 0`.

That determinant is the characteristic polynomial, a degree-`n` polynomial in `lambda`. Its roots
are the eigenvalues; for each, the null space of `A - lambda*I` is the eigenspace. An eigenvalue's
multiplicity as a root is its algebraic multiplicity; the dimension of its eigenspace is its
geometric multiplicity, and the second never exceeds the first.

```python
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[2.0, 1.0],
              [1.0, 2.0]])
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eig(A)
assert np.allclose(sorted(vals), [1.0, 3.0])
for i in range(2):
    assert np.allclose(A @ vecs[:, i], vals[i] * vecs[:, i])   # A v = lambda v
```

## Diagonalisation is a change to the eigenbasis

If `A` has `n` linearly independent eigenvectors, stack them as the columns of `V` and the
eigenvalues on the diagonal of `Lambda`. Then `A V = V Lambda`, so `A = V Lambda V_inv`. Reading
right to left: `V_inv` rewrites a vector in the eigenbasis, `Lambda` scales each coordinate
independently, `V` maps back.

In that basis `A` is just `n` separate one-dimensional scalings. A matrix that lacks a full set of
independent eigenvectors is called defective and cannot be diagonalised; the smallest example is
`[[1, 1], [0, 1]]`, which has eigenvalue 1 with algebraic multiplicity 2 but only a one-dimensional
eigenspace.

```python
V, Lam = vecs, np.diag(vals)
assert np.allclose(V @ Lam @ np.linalg.inv(V), A)

defective = np.array([[1.0, 1.0], [0.0, 1.0]])
d_vals, d_vecs = np.linalg.eig(defective)
assert np.linalg.matrix_rank(d_vecs) == 1        # only one independent eigenvector
```

<Concept>

Diagonalisation says: pick the right coordinate system and the matrix stops mixing coordinates.
Everything that is hard because coordinates interact — powers, exponentials, recurrences — becomes
easy because in the eigenbasis they do not.

</Concept>

## Powers and long-run behaviour

`A^k = V Lambda^k V_inv`, and `Lambda^k` just raises each eigenvalue to the `k`-th power. So the
behaviour of `A^k` as `k` grows is governed entirely by the eigenvalues: it converges to zero
exactly when every `|lambda| < 1`, blows up if any `|lambda| > 1`, and the largest `|lambda|` (the
spectral radius) sets the rate.

The dominant eigenvector is the direction that survives repeated multiplication, which is why power
iteration converges to it and why a Markov chain's stationary distribution is the eigenvector for
eigenvalue 1.

```python
M = np.array([[0.9, 0.2],
              [0.1, 0.8]])                        # column-stochastic
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eig(M)
stationary = vecs[:, np.argmin(np.abs(vals - 1.0))].real
stationary /= stationary.sum()
assert np.allclose(M @ stationary, stationary)
```

## Trace, determinant, and complex eigenvalues

The eigenvalues carry two familiar summaries: their sum is the trace and their product is the
determinant. A real matrix can still have complex eigenvalues, always in conjugate pairs; a pure
rotation by `theta` has eigenvalues `exp(+-i*theta)` and no real eigenvector, because it turns every
real direction.

Similarity transforms `P_inv A P` preserve the eigenvalues (same characteristic polynomial) but
change the eigenvectors. This is why eigenvalues are basis-independent properties of the underlying
map, while eigenvectors depend on the coordinate system.

```python
rot = np.array([[0.0, -1.0], [1.0, 0.0]])
vals = np.linalg.eigvals(rot)
assert np.allclose(sorted(vals, key=np.imag), [-1j, 1j])
assert np.isclose(np.trace(rot), np.sum(vals).real)
assert np.isclose(np.linalg.det(rot), np.prod(vals).real)
```

## Symmetric matrices are the good case

If `A == A.T`, all eigenvalues are real, the eigenvectors can be chosen orthonormal, and
`A = Q Lambda Q.T` with `Q` orthogonal — the spectral theorem. No inverse is needed, the
decomposition always exists, and it is numerically stable.

Covariance matrices, Gram matrices, graph Laplacians, and Hessians are all symmetric, so almost
every eigen-computation in ML is really the symmetric case. Use `np.linalg.eigh`, which assumes
symmetry, returns real sorted eigenvalues, and is faster and more accurate than `eig`.

```python
S = np.array([[4.0, 1.0],
              [1.0, 3.0]])
w, Q = np.linalg.eigh(S)
assert np.allclose(Q @ Q.T, np.eye(2))           # orthonormal eigenvectors
assert np.allclose(Q @ np.diag(w) @ Q.T, S)
```

<WhyItMatters>

PCA is the eigendecomposition of a covariance matrix: eigenvectors are principal directions,
eigenvalues are variances. The eigenvalues of a loss Hessian are the local curvatures, and their
ratio is the condition number that sets gradient descent's speed. An RNN is stable only if its
recurrent weight's spectral radius is below 1. Spectral clustering uses the smallest eigenvectors of
a graph Laplacian.

</WhyItMatters>

## Doing this in floating point

Do not find eigenvalues by forming the characteristic polynomial and rooting it: converting a matrix
to polynomial coefficients is severely ill-conditioned, and polynomial root-finding is fragile.
Library routines use the QR algorithm, which iterates orthogonal similarity transforms toward a
triangular form.

For non-symmetric ("non-normal") matrices, the eigenvectors themselves can be extremely
ill-conditioned even when the eigenvalues are fine: a tiny perturbation moves them a lot, and
`V_inv` in the decomposition has a huge norm. The condition number of `V` measures this; when it is
large, prefer the Schur decomposition or the SVD for whatever you actually need.

```python
non_normal = np.array([[1.0, 1e6],
                       [0.0, 1.0 + 1e-6]])
vals, V = np.linalg.eig(non_normal)
assert np.linalg.cond(V) > 1e6                    # eigenvectors barely independent
```

<ProductionNote>

Call `eigh` for anything you know is symmetric and `eig` only when you must. Log the eigenvalue
spectrum for covariance and Hessian matrices — the ratio of largest to smallest is the condition
number driving optimisation, and a near-zero eigenvalue signals a flat direction. If you only need
the top few eigenpairs of a large matrix, use `scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh`, not a full
decomposition.

</ProductionNote>

## Cost

A full eigendecomposition of a dense `n*n` matrix is `O(n^3)`, with `eigh` roughly a constant factor
cheaper than `eig`. Matrix powers via the decomposition cost one `O(n^3)` factorisation plus
`O(n^2)` per power, versus `O(n^3)` per power by repeated multiplication — a win when you need many
powers or non-integer ones.

For a large sparse matrix, a full decomposition is out of reach; iterative methods (Lanczos for
symmetric, Arnoldi for general) find the extreme eigenpairs in a few matrix-vector products each.
Power iteration is the cheapest of all when only the dominant eigenvector is needed.

## Exercises

<Exercise id="la-eig-two-by-two" difficulty="easy">

Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of `A = [[3, 1], [0, 2]]` by hand.

<Solution>

`A` is upper triangular, so its eigenvalues are its diagonal entries: `lambda = 3` and `lambda = 2`.

For `lambda = 3`: solve `(A - 3I) v = 0`, i.e. `[[0, 1], [0, -1]] v = 0`, giving `v2 = 0` and `v1`
free. An eigenvector is `(1, 0)`.

For `lambda = 2`: solve `[[1, 1], [0, 0]] v = 0`, giving `v1 = -v2`. An eigenvector is `(1, -1)`.

Check: `A @ (1, 0) = (3, 0) = 3*(1, 0)` and `A @ (1, -1) = (2, -2) = 2*(1, -1)`. The two
eigenvectors are independent, so `A` is diagonalisable.

</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise id="la-eig-powers" difficulty="medium">

Given `A = V Lambda V_inv` with eigenvalues `0.5` and `2`, describe `A^k` as `k -> infinity` and
explain why the general term does not simply vanish or explode.

<Solution>

`A^k = V Lambda^k V_inv`, and `Lambda^k = diag(0.5^k, 2^k)`. As `k` grows, `0.5^k -> 0` while
`2^k -> infinity`, so `A^k` is dominated by the `2^k` term along the eigenvector for eigenvalue 2.

A generic vector `x` decomposes in the eigenbasis as `c1 v1 + c2 v2`. Then
`A^k x = c1 0.5^k v1 + c2 2^k v2`. The first component decays to zero; the second grows without
bound (unless `c2 = 0`). So `A^k x` neither vanishes nor stays bounded in general — it aligns with
`v2` and grows like `2^k`. `A^k` converges to zero only if every `|lambda| < 1`; here one eigenvalue
exceeds 1, so it does not.

</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise id="la-eig-defective" difficulty="hard">

Show that `A = [[2, 1], [0, 2]]` is defective, and explain what goes wrong if you try to write
`A = V Lambda V_inv`.

<Solution>

The characteristic polynomial is `det(A - lambda I) = (2 - lambda)^2`, so `lambda = 2` with
algebraic multiplicity 2.

The eigenspace is the null space of `A - 2I = [[0, 1], [0, 0]]`, which is `{(v1, 0)}`: dimension 1.
So the geometric multiplicity is 1, less than the algebraic multiplicity 2. There is only one
independent eigenvector.

To form `V` you need two independent eigenvectors as columns, but only one exists, so `V` is
singular and `V_inv` does not exist. `A` cannot be diagonalised. The closest you can do is the
Jordan form `[[2, 1], [0, 2]]` (already Jordan), or in numerical practice the Schur form, which is
upper triangular with the eigenvalue on the diagonal and always exists.

</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise id="la-eig-interview-hessian" difficulty="interview">

In an interview: "How do the eigenvalues of the loss Hessian relate to how gradient descent
behaves?"

<Solution>

Near a minimum the loss is approximately quadratic, `L(x) ~ L(x*) + 0.5 (x - x*).T H (x - x*)`, with
`H` the symmetric Hessian. In the eigenbasis of `H` the dynamics decouple: along the eigenvector for
eigenvalue `lambda`, a gradient step with learning rate `eta` multiplies the error by
`(1 - eta*lambda)`.

Convergence along that direction needs `|1 - eta*lambda| < 1`, i.e. `eta < 2/lambda`. To stay stable
in the steepest direction you must set `eta < 2/lambda_max`, and then the flattest direction
converges at rate `1 - eta*lambda_min ~ 1 - 2*lambda_min/lambda_max`. So the number of iterations
scales with the condition number `lambda_max / lambda_min`.

Practical consequences: a poorly conditioned Hessian means slow progress; momentum and Adam
effectively rescale directions to reduce the effective condition number; and a near-zero eigenvalue
is a flat valley where progress stalls. Negative eigenvalues mean a saddle, not a minimum.

</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise id="la-eig-rnn-stability-real-world" difficulty="real-world">

An RNN's hidden state explodes to `nan` after a few hundred steps during training. Relate this to
the recurrent weight matrix and give concrete mitigations.

<Solution>

Ignoring the nonlinearity, the hidden state evolves roughly as `h_t ~ W_rec^t h_0`, so its growth is
governed by the spectral radius of `W_rec` (the largest `|lambda|`). If that exceeds 1, `||h_t||`
grows geometrically and eventually overflows; the nonlinearity delays but does not prevent this when
the linearised gain is above 1.

Mitigations. Initialise `W_rec` orthogonally or with a spectral radius just below 1, so early
training is stable, and clip the global gradient norm so one bad batch cannot launch the divergence.

Use a gated architecture (LSTM, GRU) whose gates keep the effective recurrent Jacobian near 1, and
optionally apply spectral normalisation to `W_rec` using a power-method estimate of its largest
singular value. Monitor the spectral radius during training and alert if it drifts above 1.

Validate by checking that `||h_t||` stays bounded over long rollouts on held-out sequences, not just
that the `nan` disappeared on the training batch.

</Solution>
</Exercise>

## Gotchas

<Gotchas>

<Gotcha question="`np.linalg.eig` returned complex numbers for my real matrix.">

That is correct behaviour. Real matrices can have complex-conjugate eigenvalue pairs, which is how
rotation shows up. If the matrix is symmetric, use `np.linalg.eigh` instead and you will get real
eigenvalues.

</Gotcha>

<Gotcha question="I used `eig` on a symmetric matrix and the eigenvectors are not orthogonal.">

`eig` does not exploit symmetry and can return a slightly non-orthogonal, unsorted set with tiny
imaginary parts. `eigh` guarantees real, sorted eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors, and is
faster. Always use `eigh` for symmetric input.

</Gotcha>

<Gotcha question="My matrix has a repeated eigenvalue, so it must be defective.">

Not necessarily. A repeated eigenvalue is defective only if its eigenspace is smaller than its
multiplicity. The identity matrix has eigenvalue 1 repeated `n` times and is perfectly
diagonalisable. Check the geometric multiplicity.

</Gotcha>

<Gotcha question="The eigenvalues look right but the eigenvectors change wildly with a tiny input change.">

For a non-normal matrix the eigenvectors can be ill-conditioned even when the eigenvalues are not.
`np.linalg.cond(V)` will be large. Use the Schur decomposition or the SVD for whatever you need
downstream.

</Gotcha>

<Gotcha question="Eigenvectors from `np.linalg.eig` have inconsistent signs and scale between runs.">

Eigenvectors are defined only up to a nonzero scalar (a sign for real, a phase for complex).
Normalise them and fix a convention, such as making the largest-magnitude entry positive, if you
need reproducible output.

</Gotcha>

</Gotchas>

## Summary

An eigenvector is a direction `A` only scales; its eigenvalue is the scale. The eigenvalues are the
roots of `det(A - lambda*I)`, and each eigenspace is the null space of `A - lambda*I`. When there
are `n` independent eigenvectors, `A = V Lambda V_inv` changes to a basis where `A` is diagonal.

That decomposition makes `A^k = V Lambda^k V_inv`, so eigenvalue magnitudes decide whether powers
decay, blow up, or converge, and the dominant eigenvector is what power iteration and Markov chains
find. Trace is the eigenvalue sum, determinant the product; real matrices can have complex-conjugate
pairs. Defective matrices lack a full eigenbasis and have no diagonalisation.

Symmetric matrices always have `A = Q Lambda Q.T` with orthonormal `Q` and real eigenvalues — use
`eigh`. Never route through the characteristic polynomial, and watch `cond(V)` for non-normal
matrices. The Practice, Debug, Interview, Test, Project, and Review tabs build power iteration, the
decomposition, and its ML uses.
